Metal additive manufacturing is transforming the design and production of high-performance 
aerospace components. By building structures layer by layer, technologies such as laser powder 
bed fusion (LPBF) enable complex geometries that cannot be manufactured using conventional 
methods. In particular, metallic lattice structures offer exceptional opportunities for 
reducing structural weight while maintaining high stiffness and strength. These characteristics 
are highly attractive for aerospace applications, where lightweight design directly contributes 
to improved efficiency, reduced fuel consumption, and lower emissions.

Despite these advantages, the application of additively manufactured lattice structures in 
safety-critical aircraft components remains challenging. Manufacturing-induced defects, 
residual stresses, heterogeneous microstructures, and surface roughness can significantly 
reduce fatigue resistance. Moreover, the complex internal surfaces of lattice structures 
are difficult to inspect and improve using conventional finishing techniques.

<blockquote class="project-question">
  <p>How can we exploit the design freedom of metal additive manufacturing while ensuring 
  the fatigue resistance and damage tolerance required for safety-critical aerospace structures?</p>
</blockquote>

## Understanding and improving fatigue performance

SWiFT-AM addresses this challenge through an integrated research approach combining 
experimental characterization, advanced computational modelling, and innovative surface 
engineering. The project aims to establish a fundamental understanding of the relationships 
between manufacturing processes, material microstructure, structural geometry, and mechanical performance.

A major research activity focuses on understanding how LPBF processing conditions 
influence the properties of metallic lattice structures. Advanced thermo-mechanical 
simulations are developed to predict temperature evolution, residual stresses, and 
microstructural variations during manufacturing. These numerical models are supported 
by experimental investigations involving microscopy, X-ray diffraction, and mechanical 
fatigue testing.

Another important research direction concerns electrochemical polishing. This surface 
treatment offers the possibility of removing manufacturing-induced roughness and defects, 
including those located deep inside complex lattice architectures. By reducing local stress
concentrations, electrochemical polishing has the potential to delay fatigue crack
initiation and significantly improve structural reliability.

## Designing the next generation of lightweight structures

The experimental and numerical results are combined to develop physics-based design rules 
and computational optimization methods for fatigue-resistant lattice structures. A 
particularly important aspect is the balance between fatigue life and damage tolerance.
 While relatively coarse lattice structures may provide improved fatigue resistance, 
 finer architectures offer multiple load paths that can redistribute forces when 
 individual structural members fail.

SWiFT-AM investigates how this trade-off can be systematically controlled through lattice 
geometry, manufacturing parameters, and surface treatment. The resulting optimization 
framework will also enable structures with locally tailored stiffness, opening opportunities 
for compliant mechanisms and adaptive or morphing aerospace components.

<blockquote class="project-question">
  <p>SWiFT-AM aims to make fatigue resistance, damage tolerance, and structural adaptability 
  explicit design parameters, rather than limitations addressed after manufacturing.</p>
</blockquote>

## From fundamental research to aerospace applications

Ultimately, SWiFT-AM seeks to bridge the gap between fundamental materials science and 
practical aerospace engineering. The developed computational tools, experimentally validated 
models, and design guidelines will be integrated and demonstrated in a representative 
aerospace case study.

By advancing the understanding and prediction of fatigue behaviour in additively manufactured 
metals, the project will contribute to the qualification and future implementation of reliable, 
lightweight structures in aerospace systems.
